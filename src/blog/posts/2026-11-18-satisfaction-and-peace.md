As of late, I've discovered (rediscovered?) some new kind of appreciation for life. I have no idea why this is suddenly a feeling that I seem to embody, but it has been a development nonetheless, and I find it intriguing enough to write about it.

It's more than some generic feeling of happiness as well, and I feel that I know this for a fact. 2 weeks ago, I had to write 5 exams in the span of 6 days. A brutal prelim schedule indeed. I should've felt insanely stressed and objectively not happy or peaceful, and yet, the opposite was true. Somehow, every morning for the past few weeks, I've awoken with a newfound sense of purpose or joy, able to be walking up the slope, with my head bobbing around to the most random music playing from my Spotify, exactly like Mike Ross in Suits. It's an insanely surreal feeling to me, something almost extraterrestrial. For the past 5 or so years, that stress has felt almost dominating life in a sense, and I know many others feel the same. First it was doing well in school and then it was college applications and then it was actually getting to college and adapting to life here and then actually succeeding in college with clubs and applications and extracurriculars and startups and then getting internships and coffee chats and applications. A laundry list of lugubrious litter and other assorted forced tasks, one could say.

I think this experience has brought me back to the classic inquisition of what an ideal life looks like, and how that looks like for everyone. Since all reality is perception, it follows that all happiness is pure perception as well. This means that we should optimize for perceiving the highest levels of happiness possible. However, this approach cannot be correct either, and this is because of the classic diminishing returns problem. If I'm happy all the time, then the happiness will constantly mean less and less. This is why life itself is valuable - time is limited and therefore your life has intrinsic value because of the time you have left. This path of thinking has led me to the fact that an ideal life should obviously be happy, but there is some other quality of perceived "satisfaction" that we can append.

So not only does your overall level of happiness matter, but the amount of perceived satisfaction that you receive from your life overall also matters. I thus believe that the ideal true state of "peace" is one where you're not necessarily happy all the time, but where you're able to reap this satisfaction in a gratitude-like sense. You're able to go about and uniquely appreciate each day, regardless of the outcome of the day.

I had a conversation with a friend about something similar. He said that he's began approaching every day one at a time, and his actions during the day decide whether that day was a win or not, and his goal is to win every day, which is a strategy that has been working for him. I think this is somewhat effective when combined with a slight twist. While the sentiment of winning each day is a noble one, life essentially prevents every day from being a win. Maybe one day you're sick or there's an emergency. There's too much entropy in life to be able to win every day. Even as I'm writing this, I'm losing my voice due to some sickness brought on by the Ithaca cold. So there's no concrete way to win each day if you measure the quality or the relative goodness of each day by either winning or losing. Not every day is black and white. It is therefore some kind of spectrum of how good a day it was, but regardless of where it falls on the spectrum, you should be able to feel satisfaction and appreciation for that day.

This feels best explained by some kind of histogram, spanning the worst day possible to the best day possible. Most of your days will cluster somewhere around the middle. You realistically want every day to fall on the upper half of your days. To achieve this, we add some kind of base level of gratitude to every data point that I'll call the g-condition. The g-condition is a hyperbolic condition, so that the worst days get the most added, and the best days get the least added. This method pushes each day over the halfway mark on the range from worst days to best days. Claude has kindly generated a graph of this as well.

So what is the takeaway of all of this?

That you should strive towards achieving this g-condition, regardless of how your life is going, and build this satisfaction in some manner. Most people on this planet, especially those that are most likely to actually be able to read this essay, are wildly underappreciative of all of the gifts and advantages that we've been given. Appreciate the life that you've been given to the fullest, with the reminder that it can end at any moment.
